**Short answer:** owning the surface of a property does not always mean owning the minerals beneath it. A parcel can have a _split estate_, where surface ownership and mineral ownership belong to different parties. The deed history and applicable state or federal records determine what was conveyed or reserved.

## Surface estate and mineral estate

The surface estate generally covers the land at ground level and its ordinary uses. The mineral estate concerns the minerals defined by the property documents and the law that applies to the land. Those interests may be owned together or separated through a conveyance or reservation.

The phrase “mineral rights” is broad. A document may cover some minerals and reserve others, or describe rights to enter, develop, lease, or receive proceeds. The wording matters; a label alone does not establish the full scope of an interest.

The Bureau of Land Management describes federal split-estate examples in which private parties own the surface while the United States retained some or all minerals under a land patent. The BLM notes that the original patent and land status records can help identify federal mineral reservations. Other split estates are created through private deeds and reservations.

## What a split estate can mean for an owner

If you own only the surface, you may not own the oil, gas, or other minerals below it. If you own a mineral interest, you may not own the surface. The rights and responsibilities attached to each interest depend on the recorded documents, the mineral type, and state or federal law.

Mineral ownership may also be divided among multiple people. A deed, inheritance, sale, or reservation can leave a person with a fractional interest rather than ownership of the entire mineral estate. The interest shown on a royalty statement or division order is useful information, but it is not a substitute for reviewing the title documents.

## Records to start with

To understand a specific property, gather the legal description and work through the recorded history. Depending on the property, useful records can include:

- The current deed and earlier deeds that created or changed the mineral interest.
- Reservations, conveyances, leases, assignments, and recorded probate documents.
- The county and state where the property is located, along with the section, township and range or other legal description.
- Any title opinion, division order, or operator correspondence you already have.

In Texas, the Railroad Commission notes that county courthouse records may contain documents relevant to an existing lease or royalty agreement. For federal split-estate land, BLM master title plats and the original land patent may help identify federal mineral ownership; the BLM recommends contacting the local office when ownership is uncertain.

## A practical first check

Start with the deed or other document through which you received the property. Look for a mineral reservation, a separate mineral conveyance, and the exact legal description. Then compare that description with the property and county records associated with any royalty statement or operator documents.

If the chain of title is unclear, a title professional or attorney familiar with the state and county can help interpret the documents. Do not assume that surface ownership, a tax record, a lease mailing, or a single payment statement settles the full ownership question.
