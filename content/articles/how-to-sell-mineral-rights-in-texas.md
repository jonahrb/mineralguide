**To sell mineral rights in Texas, you need to verify your ownership, determine what your interest is worth, get offers from mineral buyers, choose an offer, and sign a mineral deed or other transfer document.** The transaction is generally completed when the required documents are signed, delivered, and recorded in the county where the minerals are located.

You do not have to own the surface land to sell mineral rights. Mineral ownership can be separate from surface ownership, and Texas law allows owners to sell mineral interests while retaining the surface estate.

However, selling is not your only option if you need cash. **If you want access to money but do not want to give up your mineral rights, you may be able to borrow against your mineral interest or royalty income instead.** A loan creates repayment obligations and may put pledged collateral at risk, but it can provide liquidity without an outright sale.

Here is how the selling process works.

## Step 1: Confirm that you own the mineral rights

Before selling, confirm exactly what you own.

In Texas, mineral ownership is often separated from surface ownership. A previous landowner may have sold the surface while retaining the minerals, or mineral rights may have been divided among several heirs over generations.

Start by gathering:

- The deed or other document through which you acquired the minerals.
- Probate, inheritance, or trust documents, if applicable.
- Your most recent royalty statements, if the minerals are producing.
- Existing mineral leases or division orders.
- Any previous title reports or ownership records.

If you do not have your deed, contact the county clerk in the county where the property is located. Texas county deed records contain documents affecting real property ownership.

**Important:** A royalty statement is useful evidence of an interest, but it does not necessarily establish the full legal extent of your ownership. A title search may be necessary to confirm the exact interest that can be sold.

## Step 2: Determine what your mineral rights are worth

Before accepting an offer, establish a reasonable estimate of your minerals' value.

For producing minerals, a useful preliminary guideline is **36 to 60 times your average monthly royalty income**.

For example, if your minerals generate $1,000 per month:

| Calculation | Estimated value |
|---|---:|
| $1,000 × 36 | $36,000 |
| $1,000 × 48 | $48,000 |
| $1,000 × 60 | $60,000 |

This is a screening estimate, not a guaranteed market price. Buyers also evaluate production decline, well age, location, commodity prices, lease terms, and future drilling potential.

For non-producing minerals, value depends primarily on local development potential and comparable transactions. Lease bonuses and recent mineral sales in the same area can provide useful benchmarks.

For a more detailed explanation, read [How Much Are My Mineral Rights Worth?](/learn/how-much-are-my-mineral-rights-worth).

## Step 3: Find mineral rights buyers

Once you understand what you own and have a preliminary valuation, you can begin contacting buyers.

Mineral buyers purchase interests for investment purposes. They generally evaluate the expected future income from the minerals and offer a lump sum in exchange for the rights being transferred.

You can find potential buyers through:

- Online mineral rights buying companies.
- Mineral brokers who connect owners with buyers.
- Direct outreach from mineral acquisition companies.
- Referrals from other mineral owners or industry professionals.

You do not need to accept the first offer you receive. Contacting multiple buyers is one of the best ways to determine what the market is willing to pay.

### What information will buyers need?

Most buyers will want some combination of:

- Your name and contact information.
- The county and state where the minerals are located.
- Your estimated net mineral acres or ownership percentage.
- The legal description, if available.
- Producing well names or API numbers, if applicable.
- Recent royalty statements.
- Any existing lease or title information.

You can usually begin the process without having every document. A buyer may conduct additional title research before making a final offer.

## Step 4: Compare offers carefully

Once you receive offers, do not automatically accept the highest headline number.

Make sure you are comparing offers for the same ownership interest and the same properties. One buyer may be offering to purchase all your minerals, while another may be offering to purchase only a portion.

Before accepting, compare:

| Item | What to verify |
|---|---|
| Purchase price | Total amount offered |
| Ownership transferred | Exact percentage or interest being purchased |
| Properties included | Counties, tracts, leases, and wells |
| Future royalties | Whether you give up all or only part of future income |
| Closing conditions | Title requirements and other conditions |
| Payment | Amount, timing, and method of payment |
| Closing costs | Any deductions or expenses charged to you |

**Get the purchase agreement and proposed deed before signing.** The deed determines what ownership rights you actually transfer. Do not rely only on the buyer's description of the transaction.

## Step 5: Review the mineral deed before signing

The mineral deed is one of the most important documents in the transaction.

It identifies the interest being transferred and the rights the buyer receives. Depending on the transaction, the document may transfer all of your mineral interest, a fractional share, or a specific royalty or other interest.

Pay particular attention to:

- The legal description of the property.
- Your ownership percentage or fractional interest.
- Whether the deed transfers all minerals or only specified rights.
- Whether existing leases and future royalties are included.
- Any reservations or exceptions that allow you to retain certain rights.
- Any warranties or representations you are making about ownership.

For example, selling half of your mineral interest is different from selling the entire interest. If you intend to retain 50%, the deed must accurately reflect that arrangement.

Have an attorney familiar with Texas mineral transactions review the documents if you are unsure what rights are being transferred.

## Step 6: Sign the documents, close, and receive payment

After the buyer completes its due diligence and the parties agree to the final terms, the transaction moves to closing.

A typical closing involves:

1. Finalizing the purchase agreement and deed.
2. Completing the buyer's title review.
3. Signing the required documents, including any necessary notarization or acknowledgment.
4. Delivering the signed documents according to the closing instructions.
5. Recording the deed in the appropriate county.
6. Receiving the agreed purchase payment.

In Texas, an instrument affecting real property generally must be recorded in the county where at least part of the property is located. The document must also meet the applicable execution and acknowledgment requirements for recording.

The exact timeline depends on title complexity, ownership history, and the buyer's closing process. Straightforward transactions may close relatively quickly, while inherited or divided interests can take longer.

## Can you sell only part of your mineral rights?

**Yes. You do not necessarily have to sell your entire mineral interest.**

For example, if you own 100% of an interest, you may agree to sell 50% and retain the other 50%.

This can provide a lump sum while preserving some ownership and future royalty income.

However, selling a partial interest still permanently transfers the portion described in the deed. Make sure the purchase documents specify exactly what you are keeping and what you are selling.

## Do you have to sell your mineral rights to get cash?

**No. If your primary goal is to access cash, you may be able to borrow against your mineral rights instead of selling them.**

This is an important distinction. Selling converts some or all of your ownership into cash. Borrowing provides cash now in exchange for a repayment obligation.

A mineral-backed loan may allow you to:

- Access money without selling your entire mineral interest.
- Retain the potential for future mineral ownership and appreciation.
- Address a large expense without permanently giving up the rights being financed.
- Use capital for a business, real estate investment, debt payoff, or other financial need.

Whether you can receive a loan depends on the value and type of mineral interest, royalty income, title, and the lender's requirements.

### Selling vs. borrowing against your minerals

| Consideration | Selling mineral rights | Borrowing against minerals |
|---|---|---|
| Cash received | Lump sum purchase price | Loan proceeds |
| Repayment | No loan repayment schedule | Yes, according to the loan agreement |
| Ownership | You transfer the rights described in the deed | You may retain ownership, subject to the collateral documents |
| Future royalties | You give up the income transferred to the buyer | Depends on the loan and royalty assignment terms |
| Main cost | Future ownership and income given up | Interest, fees, repayment obligations, and collateral risk |
| Best suited for | Owners who want to permanently convert minerals into cash | Owners who need liquidity but want to preserve ownership |

**The bottom line:** If you want to permanently cash out of your mineral rights, selling may be appropriate. If you want cash but would prefer to keep your minerals, investigate financing before signing a deed that permanently transfers them.

A loan is not free money. Compare the net cash you receive, total repayment, fees, collateral, and default provisions. Confirm whether the agreement is actually a loan or instead transfers ownership or assigns future production.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I sell mineral rights in Texas?

Confirm your ownership, estimate the value, contact mineral buyers, compare offers, review the purchase agreement and deed, and complete the closing. The deed should be properly executed and recorded in the county where the minerals are located.

### How long does it take to sell mineral rights?

A straightforward transaction may close in a few weeks, but complicated title or inheritance issues can extend the process. The buyer's due diligence and the time needed to establish ownership are usually major factors.

### Can I sell mineral rights without selling my land?

Yes. Texas allows mineral and surface ownership to be separated. You can sell your mineral interest while retaining ownership of the surface estate, provided you actually own the mineral interest being sold.

### Can I sell inherited mineral rights?

Yes, provided you have legally established ownership and authority to transfer the interest. You may need probate documents, recorded inheritance documents, or other title evidence before a buyer will close.

### Do I have to pay taxes when I sell mineral rights in Texas?

A sale may have federal income tax consequences. The treatment depends on the type of interest sold, your cost basis, ownership history, prior depletion deductions, and the structure of the transaction.

Texas does not have an individual state income tax, but that does not eliminate potential federal tax obligations. Consult a tax professional before closing.

### Can I sell mineral rights if they are already leased?

Yes. You can generally sell a mineral interest that is subject to an existing oil and gas lease. The buyer acquires the interest subject to the applicable lease and other existing rights. Review the deed and purchase agreement to understand which future royalties and other rights transfer.

### Can I sell my mineral rights if I only own a small percentage?

Yes. You can sell a fractional mineral interest, subject to confirming your ownership and any applicable restrictions or agreements.

### Can I borrow against my mineral rights instead of selling them?

Potentially. Some lenders provide loans secured by mineral interests or royalty income. The amount available and the terms depend on the property, ownership, production, and lender requirements.

If retaining ownership matters to you, compare financing options before agreeing to a mineral sale. Read the loan documents carefully to confirm what is pledged, how repayment works, and what happens if you default.
