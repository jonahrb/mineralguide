If your oil and gas royalty checks have decreased, there are four primary things to investigate:

1. **Lower production:** Your well produced fewer barrels of oil or Mcf of natural gas.
2. **Lower commodity prices:** The oil or gas sold for less money.
3. **Higher deductions or taxes:** More money was deducted before calculating your payment.
4. **A change to your royalty decimal:** Your ownership percentage used to calculate the payment changed.

In most cases, the explanation is lower production, lower oil and gas prices, or a combination of both.

For example, if your well produces 20% less oil and the price of oil also decreases by 10%, your royalty payment could fall by approximately 28%.

The good news is that you can usually identify the reason by comparing a few numbers on your royalty statements.

## 1. Your Well Is Producing Less Oil or Gas

This is one of the most common reasons royalty payments decrease.

Oil and gas wells generally produce the most during their early production period. As the reservoir is depleted, production typically declines over time.

This means your royalty checks can gradually decrease even if oil and gas prices remain unchanged.

For example:

| Production month | Oil production | Oil price | Gross well revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 1,000 barrels | $70 | $70,000 |
| February | 900 barrels | $70 | $63,000 |
| March | 800 barrels | $70 | $56,000 |
| April | 700 barrels | $70 | $49,000 |

If your royalty decimal remains the same, your royalty payments will decline in proportion to the reduction in revenue.

**How to identify this:** Compare the barrels of oil or Mcf of natural gas reported on your royalty statements. If production decreased while the sales price stayed relatively consistent, lower production is likely the reason your check decreased.

### Why Would Production Suddenly Drop?

Production does not always decline gradually. A well can experience a sudden reduction for several reasons:

- **Mechanical problems:** Equipment failures, pump issues, or other repairs can reduce production.
- **Well maintenance:** Operators may temporarily shut down a well to perform maintenance or workovers.
- **Pipeline or transportation problems:** Production may be restricted if oil or gas cannot be transported.
- **Weather:** Severe weather can temporarily disrupt production or transportation.
- **Operational changes:** Operators may reduce production because of changing economic or operating conditions.

A temporary production interruption can cause a significant reduction in one month's royalty payment without necessarily meaning the well has permanently declined.

## 2. Oil or Natural Gas Prices Decreased

Your royalty income is directly affected by the price your operator or purchaser receives for the oil and gas produced from your well.

Even if your well produces exactly the same amount of oil or gas every month, your royalty payment will decrease when the sales price falls.

For example:

| Production month | Oil production | Oil price | Gross well revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| January | 1,000 barrels | $80 | $80,000 |
| February | 1,000 barrels | $70 | $70,000 |
| March | 1,000 barrels | $60 | $60,000 |
| April | 1,000 barrels | $50 | $50,000 |

Production stayed exactly the same, but gross revenue fell 37.5% between January and April.

Your royalty payment would also fall approximately 37.5%, assuming your royalty decimal and other adjustments remain unchanged.

### Why Do Oil and Gas Prices Change?

Oil and gas prices fluctuate based on supply and demand.

Some of the biggest factors include:

- Global oil and natural gas production
- Demand for energy
- Economic growth or slowdowns
- Weather and seasonal heating or cooling demand
- Geopolitical events
- Pipeline capacity and regional transportation constraints

Natural gas prices can be especially volatile because of seasonal demand and regional supply constraints.

**How to identify this:** Compare the sales price per barrel or per Mcf on your royalty statements. If production stayed relatively consistent but the price decreased, commodity prices are likely responsible.

Keep in mind that the price on your royalty statement is the price received for your production, not necessarily the same as the headline WTI oil price or Henry Hub natural gas price you see in the news. Transportation, quality, processing, and regional market conditions can create differences.

## 3. Your Well Was Temporarily Shut In

A shut-in well is a well that has temporarily stopped producing.

When a well is shut in, it may generate little or no production revenue during that period. Consequently, your royalty check may be substantially smaller or even zero.

Common reasons include:

- Equipment repairs
- Pipeline maintenance
- Mechanical failures
- Operator maintenance or workovers
- Unfavorable economic conditions
- Temporary transportation restrictions

A shut-in does not automatically mean the well is permanently abandoned.

If production resumes, your royalty payments may increase again.

**How to identify this:** Look for a major drop in reported production volume. If production falls to zero, check whether the operator has reported the well as inactive or whether production resumes in subsequent months.

## 4. Your Deductions Increased

Your royalty payment is generally calculated from your share of production revenue, less applicable taxes and any deductions permitted under your lease.

Depending on your lease terms, deductions may include:

- Gathering charges
- Compression costs
- Natural gas processing
- Transportation expenses

For example, suppose your gross royalty is $1,000.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Gross royalty | $1,000 |
| Gathering and transportation | -$40 |
| Processing and compression | -$30 |
| **Net payment before other adjustments** | **$930** |

If those deductions increase, your final payment decreases even if production and commodity prices stay the same.

Not every lease allows the same deductions. Some leases restrict or prohibit certain post-production costs. Review your lease before assuming a deduction is valid.

**How to identify this:** Compare the deduction lines on your royalty statements. Look for new charges, higher charges, or deductions that represent a larger percentage of your gross royalty.

## 5. Production Taxes Increased or Changed

Oil and gas production is subject to state severance taxes.

In Texas, the standard production tax rates are:

- Crude oil: 4.6% of market value.
- Natural gas: 7.5% of net taxable value.

Certain statutory exemptions and reduced tax rates may apply.

Production taxes are generally calculated as a percentage of production value. Therefore, they usually move with the value of production rather than independently causing large unexplained changes in royalty payments.

However, a change in an applicable tax exemption, tax treatment, or reported adjustment can affect the net amount you receive.

**How to identify this:** Compare the tax amounts and percentages on your royalty statements. If the tax deduction changed substantially, ask the operator or payor to explain the change.

## 6. Your Royalty Decimal Changed

Your royalty decimal represents the percentage of a well's revenue that belongs to you.

For example:

- A royalty decimal of 0.005 means you receive 0.5% of applicable revenue.
- A royalty decimal of 0.0025 means you receive 0.25% of applicable revenue.

If your royalty decimal decreases, your payment will decrease even if production and prices remain unchanged.

A decimal can change because of:

- Corrections to ownership records
- Changes to the division of interests
- Title or ownership corrections
- Changes to unit participation or allocation
- Corrections to a previous division order

For example:

| Item | Previous | Current |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gross well revenue | $100,000 | $100,000 |
| Royalty decimal | 0.005 | 0.0025 |
| **Your gross royalty** | **$500** | **$250** |

The well generated exactly the same revenue, but your royalty payment was cut in half because your decimal changed.

**How to identify this:** Compare the decimal on your current royalty statement with your previous statements and your division order. If it changed, ask the operator for a written explanation.

## 7. The Operator Made an Adjustment to a Previous Payment

Royalty payments sometimes include adjustments for previous production months.

For example, an operator may discover that it previously:

- Paid you too much.
- Paid you too little.
- Used an incorrect production volume.
- Used an incorrect sales price.
- Applied an incorrect deduction.
- Calculated your royalty using an incorrect decimal.

If the operator previously overpaid you, it may reduce a later payment to recover the overpayment, subject to applicable law and your lease.

This can make a royalty check decrease suddenly even when current production and prices have not changed.

**How to identify this:** Look for statement entries labeled adjustments, prior-period adjustments, corrections, or similar descriptions. Check whether the adjustment references a previous production month.

If you do not understand an adjustment, ask the operator to identify the production period and explain the calculation.

## How to Figure Out Exactly Why Your Royalty Check Decreased

You do not need to guess. Compare your most recent royalty statement with the previous statement and work through these five items.

| What to compare | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Production volume | Did the well produce less oil or gas? |
| Sales price | Did the operator receive less per barrel or Mcf? |
| Royalty decimal | Did your ownership percentage change? |
| Deductions and taxes | Were more expenses or taxes taken out? |
| Adjustments | Was money deducted for a previous payment period? |

Here is a simple example.

Suppose your royalty payment decreased from $1,000 to $700.

- Production decreased by 10%.
- The sales price decreased by 15%.
- Your royalty decimal stayed the same.
- Deductions did not materially change.

The combined revenue reduction is approximately 23.5%, not 25%, because the two reductions compound:

$1,000 × 0.90 × 0.85 = $765.

If your actual payment was $700, you would then investigate the additional difference through deductions, taxes, or adjustments.

## How to Check Your Well's Production in Texas

If your minerals are located in Texas, you can independently verify reported production using the Texas Railroad Commission's Production Data Query system.

You can search for production information by lease and review reported monthly oil and gas volumes.

Visit the [Texas Railroad Commission Production Data page](https://www.rrc.texas.gov/oil-and-gas/research-and-statistics/production-data/) to access production records.

There is one important timing detail: Texas production data generally has about a two-month reporting lag. Production reports can also be corrected after they are initially filed, so recent figures may change.

This means you should compare the correct production month on your royalty statement with the corresponding month in the Railroad Commission database, rather than assuming the check received this month reflects production from this month.

## When Should You Contact the Operator?

Contact the operator or royalty payor if:

- Your royalty decimal changed without an explanation.
- A new deduction appears on your statement.
- Production reported on your statement appears inconsistent with available records.
- A prior-period adjustment is unexplained.
- Your royalty payment dropped substantially without an apparent change in production or pricing.
- You stopped receiving payments despite continued production.

When contacting the operator, provide your owner number, lease or well information, and the production months in question.

Ask for a breakdown of the calculation, including production volumes, sales prices, your royalty decimal, deductions, taxes, and any prior-period adjustments.

In Texas, royalty owners also have statutory rights to certain payment information under [Texas Natural Resources Code Chapter 91](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/NR/htm/NR.91.htm).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why did my royalty check suddenly drop?

The most common explanations are a sudden reduction in production, lower oil or gas prices, a temporary well shut-in, or an adjustment to a previous payment. Compare production volume, sales price, and adjustments on your royalty statement to identify the cause.

### Do oil and gas royalty payments decrease over time?

Generally, yes. Most producing wells experience declining production as their reservoirs are depleted. Unless new wells are drilled, production improves, or commodity prices increase, royalty income from an individual well will generally decline over time.

### Can my royalty checks go to zero?

Yes. A well can temporarily stop producing, be shut in, or become uneconomic to operate. If there is no production or sale revenue attributable to your interest, you may not receive a royalty payment for that period.

### Do royalty checks increase when oil prices increase?

Yes. Assuming production volume, your royalty decimal, and other adjustments remain unchanged, higher oil prices generally increase your royalty payments.

### Why did my royalty check decrease when production stayed the same?

If production volume remained unchanged, look at the sales price, royalty decimal, deductions, taxes, and prior-period adjustments. One of these may explain the difference.

### Can an operator reduce my royalty decimal?

An operator should not arbitrarily change your ownership percentage. However, ownership records, title information, or allocation calculations may be corrected. If your decimal changes, request the documentation and calculation supporting the change.

### Why did I stop receiving royalty checks even though my well is producing?

Possible reasons include a change in the payor, an ownership or title issue, funds being held in suspense, a payment threshold, or an administrative error. Contact the operator and ask whether your account has funds in suspense and what is required to release them.

## Understand the Value of Your Mineral Rights

A decrease in monthly royalty income does not necessarily mean your mineral rights have lost their long-term value.

Individual wells naturally decline, but additional wells, future drilling, changes in commodity prices, and development activity can all affect the future income potential of your minerals.

If you own producing mineral rights, your royalty income is also an important factor in determining what your mineral interests may be worth.

If you need access to cash, selling your minerals is not your only option. You may be able to borrow against your mineral interests while retaining ownership and continuing to receive your royalty payments.
