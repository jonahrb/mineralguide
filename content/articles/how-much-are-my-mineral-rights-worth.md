**The quickest way to estimate the value of producing mineral rights is to multiply your average monthly royalty income by 36 to 60.** For example, if your mineral rights generate $1,000 per month, a reasonable preliminary estimate is $36,000 to $60,000.

That is a starting point, not a guaranteed offer. The age of your wells, production decline, location, commodity prices, and potential for additional drilling can move the actual value substantially.

If your minerals are not producing, the calculation is different. Their value is primarily based on the likelihood of future drilling and what comparable mineral interests are selling for in the area.

This guide explains how to estimate both types of mineral interests and how to determine whether an offer is reasonable.

## How much are producing mineral rights worth?

For producing mineral rights, a useful starting estimate is **36 to 60 times your average monthly royalty income**, equivalent to approximately three to five years of current royalty income.

The National Association of Royalty Owners (NARO) identifies this as a common informal valuation guideline for producing interests with a meaningful payment history.

Here is what that looks like:

| Average monthly royalties | Preliminary value estimate |
|---:|---:|
| $100 | $3,600–$6,000 |
| $250 | $9,000–$15,000 |
| $500 | $18,000–$30,000 |
| $750 | $27,000–$45,000 |
| $1,000 | $36,000–$60,000 |
| $2,000 | $72,000–$120,000 |
| $5,000 | $180,000–$300,000 |
| $10,000 | $360,000–$600,000 |

These figures are useful for getting a first estimate of what producing minerals might be worth. They are not published market prices or guaranteed buyer offers.

### How to calculate your estimate

1. Add up the royalty payments you received over the last 12 months.
2. Divide that total by 12 to calculate your average monthly income.
3. Multiply the monthly average by 36 for a conservative starting estimate.
4. Multiply it by 60 for a higher starting estimate.

**Example:**

Suppose you received $12,000 in royalties over the last 12 months.

- Annual royalty income: $12,000
- Average monthly income: $1,000
- Lower estimate: $1,000 × 36 = $36,000
- Higher estimate: $1,000 × 60 = $60,000

Your preliminary mineral-rights value is $36,000 to $60,000.

Use actual royalty statements rather than assuming your latest check represents a typical month. Oil and gas payments can fluctuate because of commodity prices, production volumes, downtime, and deductions.

## What makes mineral rights worth more or less?

The valuation multiple is not the same for every property. Two mineral owners receiving identical royalty checks can have very different asset values because their wells may have very different production histories and future potential.

These are the biggest factors:

### 1. Age of the wells and production decline

Newer wells generally have more remaining production potential than older, declining wells.

For example, consider two owners who each receive $1,000 per month:

- **Owner A:** The wells recently started producing and have a substantial amount of expected production remaining.
- **Owner B:** The wells have been producing for 15 years and are nearing the end of their economic lives.

Both receive the same royalty check today, but Owner A's mineral interest may be worth considerably more because the income is expected to continue for longer.

This is why buyers examine historical production and estimated future production instead of simply multiplying the latest royalty check.

### 2. Location and future drilling potential

Mineral rights in active oil and gas areas can be worth more because operators may develop additional wells on or near the acreage.

For example, acreage in an active part of the Permian Basin may have additional development potential that acreage in a mature or inactive area does not.

However, nearby drilling activity is not a guarantee that your minerals will be developed. The formation, exact location, operator plans, spacing, and economics all matter.

### 3. Oil and natural gas prices

Higher commodity prices generally improve the economics of producing wells and can increase the value of mineral interests.

Oil and gas prices also affect future production forecasts. A buyer evaluating your minerals will generally use supportable long-term price assumptions rather than assuming today's prices will continue indefinitely.

### 4. Royalty rate and lease terms

Your royalty rate determines the share of production revenue attributable to your interest under the lease.

A higher royalty rate generally produces more income from the same amount of production. However, deductions for gathering, processing, transportation, and other costs can reduce the amount you actually receive.

The important number for valuation is the income attributable to the specific interest you own—not simply the well's total revenue.

### 5. Ownership and title

The exact interest you own matters.

A mineral owner may own a fraction of the minerals under a property, a royalty interest, an overriding royalty interest, or another specific interest. These are not interchangeable.

Unclear ownership records, unresolved inheritance issues, existing liens, or title defects can delay a transaction and affect what a buyer is willing to pay.

## How much are non-producing mineral rights worth?

**Non-producing mineral rights cannot be valued using royalty income because they are not currently generating royalties.** Their value comes primarily from the likelihood of future development.

A useful informal starting point is approximately **three times the going lease bonus in the area**, provided there is credible, recent leasing activity for comparable acreage.

For example, if comparable mineral owners in your area are receiving a $500-per-net-mineral-acre lease bonus, the three-times guideline suggests a preliminary benchmark of $1,500 per net mineral acre.

This is only a screening method. It is not a universal price-per-acre formula.

The most important distinction is whether your minerals are leased, unleased, or leased but not producing:

- **Leased and producing:** Estimate value using royalty income and future production.
- **Leased but not producing:** Evaluate the lease terms, drilling commitments, nearby activity, and likelihood of development.
- **Unleased:** Evaluate the geological potential, recent leasing activity, operator interest, and comparable transactions.

A mineral interest in an active drilling area can be worth substantially more than an otherwise similar interest in an area with little development activity.

### What is a net mineral acre?

A net mineral acre (NMA) represents your ownership share of the mineral acreage.

For example, if you own 25% of the minerals under 100 gross acres, you own 25 net mineral acres.

This distinction is important because mineral buyers generally compare acreage values using net mineral acres rather than gross property acreage.

## How do mineral buyers determine what to offer?

Professional mineral valuations generally use two primary approaches:

**1. Income approach**

For producing minerals, buyers estimate the future royalty income the interest is expected to generate and calculate its present value.

This typically involves examining:

- Historical oil and gas production.
- Expected production decline.
- Oil, natural gas, and natural gas liquids prices.
- Royalty rates and deductions.
- Existing wells and potential future drilling.
- The risk and timing of future income.

Future income is discounted because money received years from now is worth less than money received today, and future production is uncertain.

**2. Market approach**

Buyers also examine comparable mineral transactions in the same area.

For producing interests, this may include comparing royalty income and transaction multiples. For non-producing interests, buyers may compare recent sales on a net-mineral-acre basis.

Comparable transactions are most useful when the properties have similar geology, lease terms, production, ownership, and development potential.

A credible valuation should explain the assumptions behind the number rather than simply provide a price.

## How can I find out what my mineral rights are worth?

If you want to get a realistic estimate, gather the following information:

1. **County and state:** Where the mineral interest is located.
2. **Ownership amount:** Your net mineral acres or exact fractional ownership, if known.
3. **Production information:** Names of producing wells and operators, if available.
4. **Royalty statements:** Ideally, the last 12 months of royalty payments.
5. **Lease information:** Whether the minerals are leased and the royalty rate, if known.
6. **Existing offers:** Any written offers you have received.

You do not need to have every document to start researching the value. Recent royalty statements and the county where your minerals are located are a good starting point.

### Should I get multiple offers?

Yes. If you are considering selling, getting multiple offers is one of the best ways to understand what buyers are willing to pay.

Do not compare headline prices alone. Make sure each offer covers the same mineral interest, ownership percentage, properties, and future rights. Review title requirements, closing conditions, deductions, and payment terms.

An unsolicited offer is not necessarily the market value of your minerals. It is simply one buyer's proposed price.

## Should I sell my mineral rights or keep them?

Knowing what your minerals are worth is only half the decision. The other question is whether selling is the best way to access that value.

Selling can make sense if you want a lump sum, do not want to manage uncertain future royalty income, or are comfortable transferring the rights described in the sale documents.

However, **selling is not the only way to access cash from mineral assets.**

Some mineral owners may be able to borrow against their mineral interests or royalty income instead of selling. A properly structured loan may provide access to cash while preserving the possibility of retaining ownership after repayment.

A loan is not automatically better. Interest, fees, repayment obligations, collateral, and default consequences all matter. Before signing, confirm exactly what rights are pledged and what happens if you cannot repay.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much are mineral rights worth per acre?

There is no reliable nationwide price per acre. Producing mineral interests are generally valued based on the royalty income they generate and their expected future production. Non-producing interests are valued based on local drilling potential and comparable transactions.

For non-producing acreage, recent lease bonuses and mineral sales in the same area are more useful than a national average.

### Are mineral rights worth anything if there are no producing wells?

Yes. Non-producing minerals can have value if the acreage has commercially attractive oil and gas potential or is located in an area with active leasing and drilling.

However, acreage with little geological potential or no realistic development prospects may have very little market value.

### How much are mineral rights worth if they produce $500 per month?

Using the 36-to-60-times monthly royalty guideline, mineral rights generating an average of $500 per month have a preliminary estimated value of $18,000 to $30,000.

The actual sale value may be higher or lower depending on the wells, production decline, location, and future development potential.

### Does the county determine how much mineral rights are worth?

County and location matter because geology, formations, operator activity, and drilling economics vary by area. However, two mineral interests in the same county can have very different values depending on their exact location, ownership, lease terms, and production.

### Can I get my mineral rights appraised?

Yes. A qualified mineral appraiser or valuation professional can prepare a formal valuation. Petroleum engineers may also prepare reserve reports or economic evaluations that estimate future production and income.

A formal appraisal is particularly useful for estate planning, probate, tax matters, litigation, or other situations where documented fair market value is required.

### Can I borrow against my mineral rights instead of selling them?

Potentially. Some lenders offer financing secured by mineral interests or royalty income. Approval, loan amount, repayment terms, and collateral requirements vary by lender and property.

Before accepting financing, review the agreement carefully to determine whether it creates a loan, assigns royalty payments, transfers any ownership, or combines these features.
